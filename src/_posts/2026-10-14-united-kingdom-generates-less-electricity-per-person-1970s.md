summary: >-
  While global per-capita electricity generation has risen by over 50% this
  century, the United Kingdom's output peaked at roughly 6,700 kWh per person
  in 2003 and has fallen by more than a third to about 4,200 kWh, below
  late-1960s levels. Rising net electricity imports, which grew from 1% of
  demand in 2010 to 9% in 2025, explain only a small fraction of the drop.
  International Energy Agency data shows industrial electricity consumption
  fell by a quarter since 2005 as manufacturing gave way to services, yet
  residential and commercial demand fell by just as much. Efficiency gains
  like LED lighting and stricter appliance standards drove much of the
  reduction alongside deindustrialization.
---

Many commenters view the drop not as an efficiency triumph but as demand
destruction driven by some of the world's highest electricity prices—around
$0.33 per kWh—and decades of deindustrialization that hollowed out British
steel, chemicals, and glass manufacturing. Critics blame marginal-cost wholesale
pricing tied to expensive peak gas, grid transmission bottlenecks, and
inflation-linked Contracts for Difference that pushed older offshore wind strike
prices like Dudgeon from £161.71 to £220.95 per MWh. The sharpest objection is
that UK grid reliability is far better than during the 1974 Three-Day Week
rationing, and that Britain's industrial decline began decades before recent
energy price spikes. Defenders of the current trajectory add that newer wind
contracts clear closer to £80–£90 per MWh and that better insulation and
appliance standards cut waste without lowering living standards.
