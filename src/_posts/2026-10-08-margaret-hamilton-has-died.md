summary: >-
  Margaret Hamilton, who led the team that wrote NASA's Apollo flight software
  at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory, died on Sep. 30 at 90. MIT's obituary
  follows her from weather-prediction code for Edward Lorenz and the SAGE
  air-defense system to becoming Apollo's first programmer, leading 400
  people. It credits her with defensive programming — she proposed guarding
  the P01 program after her daughter crashed a simulator, a mistake Jim Lovell
  repeated on Apollo 8 — and with the priority scheduling that shed background
  tasks when the 1202 alarm fired during the Apollo 11 landing. She coined
  'software engineering' to legitimize the field and received the Presidential
  Medal of Freedom in 2016.

**On Hacker News.** Most commenters land on Hamilton as deserving HN's black
mourning bar, because she turned software reliability into a discipline when the
field was still treated as art and magic, and several highlight her fight to
coin 'software engineering' plus her Computer History Museum oral history. The
sharpest objection comes from setr, who argues the famous Apollo 11 1202 alarm
is oversold as proof of 'man-rated' robustness: a computer refusing an eighth
job when only seven fit would be ordinary error handling, not foresight. The
reply from jonstewart, bronson and jacquesm is the constraint itself — a
priority scheduler with interrupts running on 4KB of RAM at 0.043 MIPS, with ROM
sewn by hand and no prior art to copy. msl adds that the tidy telling is not
even true: the real cause of the 1201 and 1202 alarms is considerably more
complicated than a full task table. A smaller fight breaks out over whether
'software engineer' should be gatekept, with customguy arguing it requires
externally imposed rules and altmanaltman insisting terms simply change.

**On Lobsters.** Most commenters land on Hamilton as an uncompromising figure in
computing history, with the top-rated reply simply calling her a legend and
linking the Lego Women of NASA set that sits on the commenter's desk. The one
substantive exchange is about the term she made respectable. Koala, professing a
complex relationship with "software engineering," quotes her own admission that
she adopted the phrase to win a discipline nobody took seriously, since it was
"an ongoing joke" when she started using it in the 1960s. Amw-zero answers that
the label genuinely fits some of the work and not other parts, comparing
programmers to carpenters and electricians rather than structural engineers, and
naming capacity planning and performance work as the parts that count. Nobody
disputes her Apollo record; the argument is only whether the profession she
named describes what most of its members actually do.
